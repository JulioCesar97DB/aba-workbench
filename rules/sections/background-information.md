# Section 3: Background Information

## What this section is for

It introduces the child to a reader who has never met them: who the child is, who they live with, how they came to be diagnosed, what has happened in their development and health, where they go to school, what help they already receive, and what brought the family to ABA. Every later section leans on it: the reviewer reads Medical Necessity, the behaviors and the plan against this picture, so names, dates, people and services set here must be the ones the rest of the assessment uses.

Weak versions read like a form filled in sentence by sentence, could describe any child with the same diagnosis, leave out who reported each fact, or slip in history the sources do not give.

## What it covers

Her assessments give this section about 6 paragraphs, in this order, as far as the sources give each topic:

1. The household: who the child lives with, siblings, the languages spoken at home and the child's own, how the parents are involved, and what the family wants from treatment (its priorities). The priorities set here are the ones Medical Necessity, Strengths and Weaknesses and Family/Caregiver Involvement come back to.
2. The diagnosis as the CDE states it (the diagnosis, who made it and when), without its severity level, which Medical Necessity states, current medications as reported, pregnancy and birth when reported, medical history and the specialists who follow the child.
3. Developmental milestones (usually from the CDE) and how the child's communication developed.
4. School: the program or grade, the schedule and the school's plan (IEP or ESE plan); earlier therapies and services, and any earlier ABA and why it ended. Speech or occupational therapy the school gives under the child's ESE plan is described as part of the school's plan, not as a separate service the family receives, because it is not a paid service. Other therapies belong here, not in another section.
5. The concerns that led to this assessment, in daily living and in behavior, each attributed to its source (the caregivers, the CDE, the teacher) and in their terms.
6. Social life and play, sensory responses and how the child handles transitions. A topic the family raises that is not a target of treatment (for example a narrow diet) can be named as such, attributed, so the reader knows it was considered.

Cite the source inside the sentence. Write connected paragraphs, not one sentence per item.

## Length

About 6 paragraphs and 600 to 900 words.

## What it owns, and what it leaves to other sections

This section owns the child's history and circumstances. Other sections refer to these facts; they do not retell them, so state them fully and once here.

It holds no scores: no Vineland-3, BASC-3 PRQ or CDE test scores, and the diagnosis is stated without its severity level or the rest of the CDE's findings. Medical Necessity owns the standardized results.

- The concerns that led to the referral are given by source, as concerns. Their counts, baselines, functions and risks belong to Medical Necessity, the behavior sections and Risk Assessment. An incident is told here only when it is the reason for the referral, and then briefly.
- Changes happening now or coming (a new school, a move, a change of caregiver) belong to Environmental Changes. Mention a change here only when it is part of the child's history.

## Gaps in this section

- Leave out a topic the sources say nothing about, unless the payer facts make it expected (the diagnosis and who made it, other services the child receives); then mark it [MISSING: ...]. When the sources themselves say something is unknown or was not available, say so plainly.
- When two sources disagree on a date, a name, a service or a description of the child, set the two accounts side by side in the sentence, each attributed, or mark it [CHECK: ...] when the difference matters to the plan.
