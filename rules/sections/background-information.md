# Section 3: Background Information

## What this section is for

It introduces the child to a reader who has never met them: who the child is, who they live with, how they came to be diagnosed, what has happened in their development and health, where they go to school, what help they already receive, and what the family wants from treatment. Every later section leans on it: the reviewer reads Medical Necessity, the behaviors and the plan against this picture, so the people and services set here must be the ones the rest of the assessment uses.

Weak versions read like a form filled in sentence by sentence, could describe any child with the same diagnosis, leave out who reported each fact, or slip in history the sources do not give.

## What it covers

Her assessments give this section about 6 paragraphs, in this order, as far as the sources give each topic:

1. The household: who lives in the home, who cares for the child day to day, who works and supports the household (that they work, not their hours: the hours and availability belong to Family/Caregiver Involvement), siblings, the family's culture and background (for example a Latino family, a Haitian family), the languages spoken at home and the child's own, how the caregivers are involved, and the goals the family wants the child to reach (its priorities). When the family asks for therapy in another language, say so here, in this first paragraph; the rest of the assessment is in English. The priorities set here are the ones Medical Necessity, Strengths and Weaknesses and Family/Caregiver Involvement come back to.
2. The diagnosis by its name as the CDE writes it, and that the CDE made it, never with a severity level; current medications as reported; pregnancy and birth when reported; medical history and the specialists who follow the child, each by role.
3. Developmental milestones (usually from the CDE) and how the child's communication developed.
4. School: the program or grade, the schedule and the school's plan (IEP or ESE plan); earlier therapies and services, and any earlier ABA and why it ended. Speech or occupational therapy the school gives under the child's ESE plan is described as part of the school's plan, not as a separate service the family receives, because it is not a paid service. Other therapies belong here, not in another section.
5. The concerns about daily living and behavior, each attributed to its source (the caregivers, the CDE, the teacher) and in their terms.
6. Social life and play, sensory responses and how the child handles transitions. A topic the family raises that is not a target of treatment (for example a narrow diet) can be named as such, attributed, so the reader knows it was considered.

Cite the source inside the sentence, without dates. Write connected paragraphs, not one sentence per item.

## What it leaves out

- The reason for referral: the agency gives it. Do not write it, and do not write who referred the child or when the order was signed.
- The CDE's tests: do not name them (for example ADOS-2 or WISC-V) or give their results. From the CDE, take what bears on the behaviors and the child's history: the diagnosis, the milestones, the family history.
- Scores of any kind, and the severity level.
- Sensitive family matters (custody, DCF involvement, violence, immigration status): only when they affect treatment, and then in neutral terms, without detail beyond what the plan needs.
- Names: caregivers by relation, professionals by role, the child by first name only. Never a date of birth, an address or a member ID.

## Length

About 6 paragraphs and 600 to 900 words.

## What it owns, and what it leaves to other sections

This section owns the child's history and circumstances. Other sections refer to these facts; they do not retell them, so state them fully and once here.

- The concerns are given by source, as concerns. Their counts, baselines, functions and risks belong to Medical Necessity, the behavior sections and Risk Assessment. An incident is told here only briefly, when it is why the family sought help.
- Changes happening now or coming (a new school, a move, a change of caregiver) belong to Environmental Changes. Mention a change here only when it is part of the child's history.
- Medications are stated here and also go in the Diagnosis Code table, which the agency fills in.

## Gaps in this section

- Leave out a topic the sources say nothing about, unless the payer facts make it expected (the diagnosis, other services the child receives); then mark it [MISSING: ...]. When the sources themselves say something is unknown or was not available, say so plainly.
- When two sources disagree on a name, a service or a description of the child, set the two accounts side by side in the sentence, each attributed. This is the only section that does; elsewhere a disagreement is a [CHECK: ...] question for her.
