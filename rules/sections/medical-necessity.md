# Section 4: Medical Necessity Criteria

From the case map, this section uses the client (the diagnosis and the other services); the deficits with their scores; the behaviors (names and baselines, the most impairing argued with their incidents); the settings; the strengths, only to qualify. The other parts only keep names and numbers the same.

## What this section is for

This is where the reviewer decides. The section must show, for this child, that the deficits are real (standardized scores), that they impair major life activities in more than one setting (the CDE, the parents' report, direct observation, the school), and that ABA is the right treatment to change them. The rest of the assessment details the plan; this section makes the case for it.

The BCBA considers it the most important section of the assessment and the one that fails most often. Weak versions list test names and scores without explaining them; repeat the diagnosis and declare that "ABA is medically necessary" without connecting the evidence to that conclusion; could have been written about any child; blur which source said what; stay inside the home; or make up incidents and numbers to sound specific. The reviewer is technical: never explain a term.

## Required structure

This structure is the BCBA's own and it is not optional. Follow it in this order, in plain paragraphs.

1. Opening paragraph. Name everything used to establish the clinical need for ABA, by source and without dates or names: the Comprehensive Diagnostic Evaluation (CDE) and the role of who completed it, the Vineland-3 Comprehensive Parent/Caregiver Interview and its respondent by relation, the BASC-3 PRQ and its respondent, the parent interview, direct observation and its settings, and the IEP or teacher reports when there are any. State the diagnosis by its name as the CDE writes it. Never state, mark or mention a DSM-5 severity level or "requiring support" wording, whether or not the CDE gives one. Then say, in a sentence of your own, what these sources show together: the areas where deficits were found, for example self-care and daily living, communication, coping with change and with demands, safety, and participation with peers and in the community.

2. Vineland-3 paragraph. Give the deficits with the specific scores, written into prose rather than listed: the Adaptive Behavior Composite and each domain's standard score with its percentile and descriptive level, compactly; the subdomain v-scale scores with their levels, leading with the lowest and what they cover, and any domain or subdomain that stands out; and, when reported, the Maladaptive Behavior Index with the Internalizing and Externalizing v-scale scores and their levels, and the critical items the report lists. Say what the numbers mean in plain words compared with same-age peers (from a printed percentile only), and tie them at once to what the caregiver reported, so the scores read as this child's daily life. Use exactly the numbers on the score report.

3. One paragraph per deficit area named in the opening paragraph, in the same order. The areas she usually argues, when the sources support them: self-care and daily living, communication, coping with change and with demands, safety, and participation with peers and in the community. Build each paragraph from: the deficit; one concrete example from this child's life, reported or observed, told well, with its numbers (frequency, duration, setting, who was present); why it limits the child's independence, safety or participation; and what ABA will target for it, stated as what the child will learn to do (for example, to share materials with a peer rather than grab them). One example per paragraph: choose the one that best shows the impact, and leave the others to the sections that own them. Name no procedure, program or support unless her sections, her standing instructions, her notes or a source name it (see the four kinds of content), and never name extinction or a punishment procedure. Where the family's priorities (from Background Information) bear on an area, say so. Where the BASC-3 PRQ bears on an area (usually how behavior affects the caregivers), say what it shows that supports the need, in words, without its T-scores: the scores belong to Assessments Conducted.

4. Direct observation paragraph. What direct observation showed, as its own paragraph: the settings, what the child was observed doing, and what it shows that the reports alone do not (a behavior seen as the caregivers describe it, a skill seen in use, or a difference between settings). Write it from the observation ("During direct observation in the home, Nadia was observed ..."), never "the BCBA observed" or "the BCBA saw", and without dates. The areas' paragraphs may already use one observed example each; this paragraph gives the observation as evidence in its own right without retelling them.

5. Closing paragraph. Conclude that these findings establish the medical necessity of an ABA program individualized to this child. Say what ABA will address (what the child needs to learn for each deficit, and the general approach: teaching skills matched to what each behavior gets the child, and caregiver training), in which settings and why there (where the impairment shows up), the role of caregiver training (tied to what the caregivers reported and what the BASC-3 PRQ shows), and how progress will be measured: say that it is measured, and with what (direct data on target behaviors and skills, such as fewer prompts, more independent responses and more access to settings and activities, and the Vineland-3 and BASC-3 PRQ at reassessment). Always say how ABA complements, and does not duplicate, the other services the child receives (named in Background Information): school services, speech or occupational therapy, psychiatry. Do not promise outcomes. Do not state hours or units; they belong to the Summary and Recommendations section.

Be very specific throughout. Each deficit paragraph should be impossible to reuse for another child.

## Settings

Impairment must show in more than one setting. When the sources give school data (the IEP, the teacher), use them, attributed. When they give none, services will be delivered in the home as the primary setting and in the community as the secondary one: show the impairment in those two settings, with the data the sources give for each, and do not mark school data missing.

## The Florida definition, as an internal checklist

The section must meet Florida Medicaid's definition of medical necessity (Rule 59G-1.010, F.A.C., through the Florida Medicaid Definitions Policy it incorporates), but she never cites it, quotes it or names it in the text. Use it only to check that the argument is complete. In our words, the services must:

- be individualized and specific to this child, consistent with the symptoms and the confirmed diagnosis, and no more than the child needs;
- follow generally accepted professional standards, and not be experimental;
- be the level of service that can be given safely, with no equally effective, more conservative or less costly treatment available;
- not be mainly for the convenience of the child, the caregivers or the provider.

A provider's prescription or recommendation does not by itself make a service necessary, so the argument rests on this child's evidence. (The definition's first condition, protecting life, preventing significant illness or disability or relieving severe pain, applies only to recipients aged 21 or older, so it is not part of this check for her clients.) If a point is not supported by the evidence, the argument is incomplete: strengthen it with the sources' facts, never with the definition's words.

## Arguing the behaviors

The maladaptive behaviors belong in the safety or behavior paragraph, argued rather than inventoried. A paragraph that runs through every behavior with its counts reads as a list, and the reviewer learns nothing from it that the behavior sections do not already give.

- Lead with the behaviors that most limit this child's safety or participation, usually two or three. For each, give one concrete incident from the sources, then the baseline and what maintains it (from the reported and observed antecedents and consequences), and then what that means: who is at risk, what the child misses or cannot do, where.
- A threat, an injury, a near miss or a lost day of school in the sources weighs more than another count. Use it.
- Name the other targeted behaviors by name only; their baselines are in the behavior sections. Give a baseline only for a behavior the paragraph argues.
- Never give FAST or MAS results here: they belong to Assessments Conducted and Hypothesis Based Interventions. Write the function as what maintains the behavior ("is maintained by escape from demands"), never "hypothesized".
- When the behaviors are many or of different kinds (aggression toward others, leaving the area, self-injury), two paragraphs read better than one long one, as long as the section stays within 10 paragraphs. Each still follows the order above.
- Repetitive movement or stereotypy is not, by itself, a reason to reduce a behavior. Argue it only where the sources show it interrupting learning, a transition, a shared activity or safety, and say that this is why it matters; otherwise leave it out or say plainly that it is not a target.

## Explaining a number

The style guide says how every number is explained. Here in particular: "A Daily Living Skills standard score of 70" says nothing to a reviewer until the sentence goes on to what that looks like in this child's day, and a score explained in the Vineland-3 paragraph is not restated in an area's paragraph.

## Length

Her recent assessments run 8 or 9 paragraphs and about 950 to 1,150 words; aim for that, depending on how many deficit areas there are, and stay within 7 to 10 paragraphs and 900 to 1,250 words. Aim for density, not length: every sentence should carry a fact, its source, or what the fact means for the child.

When she asks for a shorter version, cut restated numbers, repeated points and general sentences first; keep the concrete incidents, which carry the argument.

## Gaps in this section

- A missing score, percentile or level on the Vineland-3 report stays visible as [MISSING: ...]. The instrument is required by the payer, so the BCBA needs to see the gap before submitting.
- If a setting is described only second-hand (a parent's account of school), say whose account it is.
- If the CDE lacks something the payers expect of it (see the payer facts), do not supply it; mark it. The severity level is the exception: she never states or marks it.
- When two sources disagree on a fact this section uses, do not set both in the text: mark it [CHECK: ...] with the question of which to keep.
