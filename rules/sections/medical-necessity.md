# Section 4: Medical Necessity Criteria

## What this section is for

This is where the reviewer decides. The section must show, for this child, that the deficits are real (standardized scores), that they impair major life activities in more than one setting (the CDE, the parents' report, direct observation, the school), and that ABA is the right treatment to change them. The rest of the assessment details the plan; this section makes the case for it.

The BCBA considers it the most important section of the assessment and the one that fails most often. Weak versions list test names and scores without explaining them; repeat the diagnosis and declare that "ABA is medically necessary" without connecting the evidence to that conclusion; could have been written about any child; blur which source said what; stay inside the home; or make up incidents and numbers to sound specific.

## Required structure

This structure is the BCBA's own and it is not optional. Follow it in this order, in plain paragraphs. Her answers of 2026-11-21 gave four parts; her recent assessments add a paragraph of their own for the direct observation, which is part 4 here.

1. Opening paragraph. Name everything used to establish the clinical need for ABA: the CDE (evaluator, credentials and date as given), the Vineland-3 Comprehensive Parent/Caregiver Interview (respondent and date), the BASC-3 PRQ (respondent and date), the FBA questionnaires, the parent interview, the BCBA's direct observations (dates and settings), and the IEP or teacher reports when there are any. State the diagnosis as the CDE states it. Then say, in a sentence of your own, what these sources show together: the areas where deficits were found, for example self-care and daily living, communication, coping with change and with demands, safety, and participation with peers and in the community.

2. Vineland-3 paragraph. Give the deficits with the specific scores, written into prose rather than listed: the Adaptive Behavior Composite and each domain's standard score with its percentile and adaptive level, compactly; then the relative weaknesses and strengths (the lowest subdomain v-scale scores and what they cover, and any domain or subdomain that stands out as a strength); and, when reported, the Maladaptive Behavior Index with the Internalizing and Externalizing v-scale scores and their levels. Say what the numbers mean, in plain terms, compared with same-age peers, and tie them at once to what the caregiver reported, so the scores read as this child's daily life. Use exactly the numbers on the score report.

3. One paragraph per deficit area named in the opening paragraph, in the same order. The areas she usually argues, when the sources support them: self-care and daily living, communication, coping with change and with demands, safety, and participation with peers and in the community. Argue each deficit with what the CDE, the parents' report and the BCBA's direct observation say about it, plus the IEP or teacher reports when available. Build each paragraph from: the score or finding; a concrete incident or observation from this child's life, with its numbers (frequency, duration, setting, who was present); why it limits the child's independence, safety or participation at home, at school or in the community; and what ABA will target for it, naming the skill or the procedure (for example, differential reinforcement of an alternative behavior to replace grabbing a peer's materials). One concrete example per paragraph is usually enough; choose the one that best shows the impact. Where the family's priorities (from Background Information) bear on an area, say so. Bring in the BASC-3 PRQ where it bears on an area, usually how behavior affects the caregivers, not only in the closing.

4. Direct observation paragraph. What the BCBA saw during her own observations, as its own paragraph: the settings and dates, what the child did, and what it shows that the reports alone do not (a behavior seen as the caregivers describe it, a skill seen in use, or a difference between settings). The areas' paragraphs may already use one observed example each; this paragraph gives the observation as evidence in its own right without retelling them.

5. Closing paragraph. State, in your own words, that individualized ABA is medically necessary for these findings. Explain how ABA will improve these specific deficits (name the main skills it will teach and how it will teach them), in which settings and why there (where the impairment shows up), the role of caregiver training (tied to the BASC-3 PRQ results and what the parents reported), coordination with the school or other providers when they are part of the picture, and how progress will be judged (direct data on target behaviors and skills, such as fewer prompts, more independent responses and more access to settings and activities, and the Vineland-3 and BASC-3 PRQ at reassessment). Do not promise outcomes. Do not state hours or units; they belong to the Summary and Recommendations section.

Be very specific throughout. Each deficit paragraph should be impossible to reuse for another child.

## Arguing the behaviors

The maladaptive behaviors belong in the safety or behavior paragraph, argued rather than inventoried. A paragraph that runs through every behavior with its counts and questionnaire scores reads as a list, and the reviewer learns nothing from it that the behavior sections do not already give.

- Lead with the behaviors that most limit this child's safety or participation, usually two or three. For each, give one concrete incident from the sources, then the baseline and the function evidence that show its size and why it continues, and then what that means: who is at risk, what the child misses or cannot do, where.
- A threat, an injury, a near miss or a lost day of school in the sources weighs more than another count. Use it.
- Name each other targeted behavior in one clause with its baseline, so the paragraph covers them all without arguing each.
- Give each questionnaire score once, where it supports the function you are explaining. Do not list FAST or MAS scores for every behavior: the complete baselines, functions and questionnaire results belong to the behavior sections and the assessments conducted.
- When the behaviors are many or of different kinds (aggression toward others, leaving the area, self-injury), two paragraphs read better than one long one. Each still follows the order above.
- Repetitive movement or stereotypy is not, by itself, a reason to reduce a behavior. Argue it only where the sources show it interrupting learning, a transition, a shared activity or safety, and say that this is why it matters; otherwise leave it out or say plainly that it is not a target.

## Explaining a number

Every score, count or duration is followed by what it means for this child: compared with same-age peers in plain words, or as what the child does or cannot yet do, where and with whom. "A Daily Living Skills standard score of 70" says nothing to a reviewer until the sentence goes on to what that looks like in this child's day. Explain each number once; do not restate the same score in two paragraphs.

## Length

Her recent assessments run 8 or 9 paragraphs and about 950 to 1,150 words; aim for that, depending on how many deficit areas there are, and stay within 7 to 10 paragraphs and 900 to 1,250 words. Aim for density, not length: every sentence should carry a fact, its source, or what the fact means for the child.

When she asks for a shorter version, cut restated numbers, repeated points and general sentences first; keep the concrete incidents, which carry the argument.

## Gaps in this section

- A missing score, percentile or level on the Vineland-3 or BASC-3 PRQ report stays visible as [MISSING: ...]. These instruments are required by the payer, so the BCBA needs to see the gap before submitting.
- If a setting is described only second-hand (a parent's account of school), say whose account it is. If a setting the argument needs is not documented at all, mark it rather than filling it in.
- If the CDE lacks something the payers expect of it (see the payer facts), do not supply it; mark it.
