# Section 6: Strengths and Weaknesses

From the case map, this section gets in full the strengths and the facts whose home is section 6. The deficit areas come by name only, in the order Medical Necessity gives them; everything else by name only too.

## What this section is for

A short narrative of what the child already does well and where the child needs support, so the reviewer and the team see the child as more than a list of deficits, and so the plan can build on the strengths. It follows Medical Necessity, which has already argued the deficits in detail: here they are explained directly, by the same names, not argued again.

## How to write it

Written as prose in 2 or 3 paragraphs (about 300 to 450 words) without bullets. It begins with the strengths, goes on to the needs, and ends with what the child needs to learn.

- No scores, percentiles or score levels, for strengths or for needs. A higher score is not in itself a strength, nor a lower one a weakness; say what the child does.
- Strengths come from the sources and are, above all, the child's emerging skills and preferred activities, items and interests that treatment can use as reinforcers or as a context for teaching other skills: what caregivers, teachers, the CDE or direct observation show the child does well, likes, or does on their own. Each strength is concrete (what the child does, where, with whom) and tied to how treatment can use it.
- When the BASC-3 PRQ shows a good result for the caregivers, name it as a strength, in words, and say what it means for treatment.
- The family's involvement is a strength: say in one clause that it will help carry out the plan and generalize its strategies across settings, especially when services will also be given at school or in a center. Who takes part and their willingness are Family/Caregiver Involvement's to state; do not restate them here, and refer to the family's priorities and household in a clause, never in Background Information's words.
- Where a strength does not hold consistently, qualify it: "Although [name] can ..., [he/she] does not yet ... without ...". The qualification and the use in treatment are facts from the sources or plain inferences from them. Do not link a strength to a deficit by your own inference.
- The needs are the deficit areas named in Medical Necessity, in the same order and with the same terms, each in a sentence or two saying what the child does not yet do, with its source. Use examples other than the ones Medical Necessity used, so each section has its own support; when the sources give no other, say the same need in other words, without retelling its incident or its numbers.
- Close with a sentence or two on what ABA will address: what the child needs to learn for each need, stated as its plain reverse, in line with Medical Necessity's closing and the family's priorities. Name no procedure, program, strategy or support (see the four kinds of content).
- Do not turn a deficit into a strength, or praise without a source.
