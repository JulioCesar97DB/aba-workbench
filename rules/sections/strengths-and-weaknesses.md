# Section 6: Strengths and Weaknesses

## What this section is for

A short narrative of what the child already does well and where the child needs support, so the reviewer and the team see the child as more than a list of deficits, and so the plan can build on the strengths (preferred activities, skills that can carry new learning). It follows Medical Necessity, which has already argued the deficits in detail: here they are summarized, not argued again, with the same names and numbers.

## How to write it

- Strengths come from the sources: what caregivers, teachers, the CDE or the BCBA's observations say the child does well, likes, or does independently, and the higher areas on the standardized reports. Each strength is concrete (what the child does, where, with whom).
- State a strength and then qualify it where it does not hold consistently: "Although [name] can ..., [he/she] does not yet ... without ...".
- Weaknesses are the deficit areas named in Medical Necessity, in the same order and with the same terms, each in one or two sentences tied to its source.
- Do not turn a deficit into a strength, or praise without a source.

## Not yet settled with the BCBA

The decisions file only says this section is a narrative. It does not say how long it is, whether strengths and weaknesses go in separate paragraphs, whether preferences and reinforcers belong here or only in the preference assessment, or whether the weaknesses should repeat scores. These rules keep to what the whole-assessment rules already require; ask her before adding more.
