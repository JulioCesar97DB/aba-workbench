# Section 19: Risk Assessment

## What this section is for

It states the risks the assessment found, to the child, to others and to the success of the plan, so the team and the reviewer see what the plan and the crisis plan protect against. The payer facts list safety among the impairments that qualify for services: aggression, self-injury, property destruction, elopement, and difficulty recognizing risk or danger.

## What to write

Prose, 3 or 4 paragraphs and about 300 to 400 words, with no risk rating. In her assessments the order is:

1. Risks to the plan itself: what could keep it from working for this child, as the sources point to it (for example cues that differ from one caregiver or setting to another, or a child who waits for prompts), stated as reasoning from the facts.
2. The risks from the behaviors, in prose, starting with the most dangerous and ending with those that interfere only with learning or participation: each behavior as the assessment names it, how often and how intensely it occurs, where and with whom, who is exposed, and any injury or near miss reported, each attributed. Risks from the child's difficulty recognizing danger (streets, water, strangers, objects) only as the sources report them.
3. How the plan answers them: with least-restrictive, function-based procedures, naming only those the sources or her sections give, and referring the reader to the Crisis Plan for the dangerous behaviors.
4. How the risk to the plan is kept small. This is her standard practice (see the four kinds of content): write it as her standing instructions for this section give it; otherwise keep the sentence and mark it [MISSING: how the risk to the plan is kept small: training, fidelity checks, adjustment].

When the sources report no risk in an area the payer facts list, do not state that the risk is absent unless a source says so.

## What it leaves to other sections

This section states risks and their evidence. What anyone does during an episode belongs to the Crisis Plan: the family's responses during a dangerous episode, de-escalation, keeping others safe, securing objects, when to seek medical care or call emergency services. Changes in the child's environment belong to Environmental Changes. A safety measure the family already took (for example, a lock added high on the front door) may be named in one clause when it bears on the size of a risk; how they respond to an episode may not. The everyday responses that caregiver training will change belong to Family/Caregiver Involvement.
