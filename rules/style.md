# How she writes

The assessment should read as if she wrote it. These are her rules and the patterns her texts show.

## Who she writes for

She writes first for the payer's clinical reviewer, a technical reader. Use the field's terms as they are; never explain a technical term in parentheses or in a gloss ("a discriminative stimulus, the cue that signals ..."). Plain does not mean simplified: it means short, exact sentences.

## Voice and person

- Plain clinical English in the third person and the active voice. Past tense for history and for what was observed, reported or scored during the assessment; present tense for the child's current functioning; "will" for what the plan does.
- The child: the first name throughout, with the pronouns the sources use. Never the child's full name.
- Caregivers: by their relation to the child ("his mother", "her grandmother") or as "the caregiver" or "the caregivers". Never a caregiver's name, given or full.
- Other professionals: by their role only ("the evaluating psychologist", "the pediatrician", "the psychiatrist", "the teacher", "the speech-language pathologist"). Never their names or credentials, even when the sources give them.
- The BCBA: always "the BCBA", in the third person; never her name or credentials, never "I" or "we".
- Never "the BCBA observed" or "the BCBA saw". What was seen in her observations is written from the observation: "During direct observation, Nadia was observed ...", "During the second observation in the home, ... was observed".
- Prose only in every narrative section: no bullets, even for a list of strengths or supports.

## Attribution

Every claim names its source in the sentence, so the kind of evidence shows: the CDE, the IEP or the teacher, the parent interview and which caregiver, the questionnaires, direct observation and its setting. Vary the form ("his mother added", "she also reported", "in the same interview", "during direct observation in the home") so the attribution does not read as a refrain.

## Dates

- No dates in narrative text. The source is enough: "During the parent interview, ...", "During direct observation, ...", "According to the CDE, ...". The dates of evaluations, interviews and observations live in Assessments Conducted, which she fills in.
- When the timing of an event in the child's history matters, the month and year or the child's age is enough ("in March 2026", "at 18 months").
- A full date only where the exact day matters to the plan, such as the day a coming change starts, and then written as "December 15, 2026". Never a numeric date ("12/15/2026", "10/05").

## Quotes

Never quote anyone. What a caregiver, a teacher, a professional or the child said is paraphrased, in English: "his mother reported that she no longer takes him to the store alone", "she threatened to hurt her brother". This holds for threats and for words in another language too. A short phrase the plan teaches the child to say is not a quote of what someone said: it may be written in quotation marks after "to say" or "to request" (for example, to say "all done").

## Abbreviations

- The first time a term with an abbreviation appears in a section, write it in full with the abbreviation in parentheses, then use only the abbreviation in the rest of that section: "functional communication training (FCT)", "Comprehensive Diagnostic Evaluation (CDE)", "Individualized Education Program (IEP)". Each section starts again, because each is pasted into its own field.
- Never spelled out, in any section: BCBA, FAST, MAS, STO, LTO, BASC-3, ABLLS-R.

## Missing and conflicting information

- What the sources do not give is stated, never guessed. When the absence is itself a fact the reader needs (an observation that could not be done, a record that was not available), say it plainly in the sentence. When a fact she must supply is missing, keep the [MISSING: ...] marker the whole-assessment rules ask for.
- In Background Information, when two sources disagree, set them side by side in one sentence, each attributed, without choosing between them.
- In every other section, do not set the two versions side by side: write the sentence with the version her sections give, or with neither, and add a [CHECK: ...] that asks her which to keep or how to resolve it ("[CHECK: the teacher reports 3 outbursts a week and the father 1 a day; which should this section use?]").

## The patterns she uses

- Deficit, example, impact, plan. Her argued paragraphs run: the deficit; one concrete example, reported or observed, from this child's life, told well; what it costs the child or the family in independence, safety or participation; and what ABA will do about it, stated as what the child needs to learn (the plain reverse of the deficit) and the general approach. Never a named procedure, program or support unless her sections, her standing instructions, her notes or a source name it (see the four kinds of content).
- Sources first, then their meaning. A paragraph that rests on several sources names them and then says, in one sentence of its own, what they show together.
- Strength, then its use or its limit. A strength is tied to how treatment will use it (a skill to build on, an interest or a preferred activity that can motivate learning), or qualified where it does not hold: "Although [name] can ..., [he/she] does not yet ... without ...". Either way the link is a fact or a plain inference from the sources, never a guess.
- A function is written as what maintains the behavior: "Elopement is maintained by access to preferred items", "is maintained by escape from demands". Never "hypothesized", "hypothesis" or "hypothetical" in a narrative section.
- A closing that ties the evidence to the request: why individualized ABA is needed, in which settings and why there, the general approach, the caregivers' part, and how progress will be judged.
- The same names everywhere: a behavior, a skill, a program or a strategy keeps one exact title in every section that names it.
- Generalization and Maintenance in exactly two paragraphs, as its rules give.

## Repetition

What she carries through several sections on purpose, and what she never repeats, is in the guide ("What she repeats on purpose, and what never").

## Explaining numbers

- Data are written in digits: counts, scores, minutes, ages ("3 times in 2 hours", "a standard score of 66", "9 years old").
- Every score, count or duration is followed by what it means: compared with same-age peers in plain words, or as what the child does or cannot do, where and with whom. A plain comparison helps the reader, such as "about 1 in 100 children his age would be expected to score this low or lower", but only when the score report prints the percentile it rests on; never work one out from a standard score.
- Give a number once, in the section that owns it. Another section that needs it names the behavior or the area and, at most, the one number its own point depends on.
- A sentence with three numbers or more and no clause saying what they mean is a list; rewrite it.

## What she avoids

- Words she does not use: "utilize", "facilitate", "leverage". Write "use", "help", "build on".
- Long dashes (— or a spaced –) inside sentences. Use a comma, a colon, parentheses or a new sentence.
- Long sentences. One idea per sentence; split a sentence that runs past about 30 words or carries more than one "which".
- Generic sentences that would fit any child ("ABA will help him reach his full potential"); each sentence carries this child's facts.
- A sales or exaggerated tone ("remarkable progress", "a comprehensive, evidence-based, individualized program").
- Text that reads as translated: Spanish word order, false friends ("realize" for "carry out", "actually" for "currently"), calques of the sources' phrasing.
- Blaming the family. Describe what the caregivers do now (giving in to end an outburst, keeping the child home) as a plain fact, without judgment; the plan's job is to give them other options.
- Treating harmless repetitive movement as a problem. Movement or stereotypy is a concern only when the sources show it interrupting learning, a transition, a shared activity or safety.
- Making speech, eye contact or an exact phrase a condition for anything; a request can be made by sign, gesture, picture or words.
- Promising outcomes. Say what the child needs to learn and how progress will be measured, not that the child will improve.
- Filler ("it is important to note", "overall", "in conclusion"), hedging stacks ("may possibly suggest") and superlatives ("extremely").
- Comments on the evidence or on the assessment itself, such as "These are school goals, not evidence that he has met them" or "Decisions will depend on whether gains hold, not on the dates of those changes". State the fact or the criterion.
- Sentences about the text ("This section describes ...") or addressed to her or to the reader.

## Terms

- Diagnoses, instruments, scales and levels exactly as the sources and score reports write them. The autism diagnosis by its name as the CDE writes it, never with a DSM-5 severity level or "requiring support" wording, in any section.
- Behaviors by the names in her list of target behaviors, the same in every section, with only the first word capitalized, in prose as in tables. When the sources use two names for one behavior, use hers and keep it. Her current names include "Elopement" (or "Bolting" for running off during shared activities), kept apart from "Leaving task area"; "Non-compliant behavior"; "Tantrum"; "Physical aggression"; "Property destruction"; "Self injury behavior"; "Repetitive motor behavior". When her list names the behavior differently, her list wins.
- A procedure, once her plan or her instructions name it, keeps that name (or the abbreviation she uses) in every section.

## Length

Each section's rules give its usual length from her texts. Within that length, shorter is better when nothing is lost: a section is too long when it retells what another section owns.
