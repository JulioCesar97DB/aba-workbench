# How she writes

The assessment should read as if she wrote it. These are the patterns her texts show; where they leave a point open, it is marked.

## Voice and person

- Plain clinical English in the third person and the active voice. Past tense for history and for what was observed, reported or scored during the assessment; present tense for the child's current functioning; "will" for what the plan does.
- The child: the first name throughout, with the pronouns the sources use.
- Caregivers: by their relation to the child ("his mother", "her parents", "the caregiver"). She uses a caregiver's own first name only rarely; do the same, and use one only when two caregivers with the same relation must be told apart.
- Professionals: by title and name as the sources give them on first mention, then by title and surname.
- The BCBA: "the BCBA", in the third person; never "I" or "we".
- Prose only in every narrative section: no bullets, even for a list of strengths or supports.

## Attribution

Every fact names its source inside the sentence, so the kind of evidence shows: the CDE and who completed it, the IEP or the teacher, the parent interview and which caregiver, the questionnaires, the BCBA's observation and its date and setting. Name the source once for a run of sentences that come from it, rather than in every sentence.

## Missing and conflicting information

- What the sources do not give is stated, never guessed. When the absence is itself a fact the reader needs (an observation that could not be done, a record that was not available), say it plainly in the sentence. When a fact she must supply is missing, keep the [MISSING: ...] marker the whole-assessment rules ask for.
- When two sources disagree, set them side by side in one sentence, each attributed, without choosing between them; mark it [CHECK: ...] when the difference matters to the plan.

## The patterns she uses

- Deficit, example, impact, plan. Her argued paragraphs run: the deficit or the score; one concrete example, reported or observed, from this child's life; what it costs the child or the family in independence, safety or participation; and what ABA will teach, named.
- Sources first, then their meaning. A paragraph that rests on several sources names them and then says, in one sentence of its own, what they show together.
- Strength, then its use or its limit. A strength is tied to how treatment will use it (a skill to build on, an interest that can motivate learning), or qualified where it does not hold: "Although [name] can ..., [he/she] does not yet ... without ...". Either way the link is a fact or a plain inference from the sources, never a guess.
- A closing that ties the evidence to the request: why individualized ABA is needed, in which settings and why there, how it will teach, the caregivers' part, and how progress will be judged.
- The same names everywhere: a skill, a program or a strategy keeps one exact title in every section that names it.
- Generalization and Maintenance in exactly two paragraphs, as its rules give.

## What she repeats, and what she never repeats

- On purpose, she carries two things through several sections: the family's priorities (from Background Information into Medical Necessity, Strengths and Weaknesses and Family/Caregiver Involvement), and the safety response for dangerous behaviors (from the interventions into Risk Assessment and the Crisis Plan). The other sections point back to them in a sentence; they do not retell the evidence.
- She never repeats score tables or a run of scores, and she never retells an incident or a session note in every section. Each fact has one home (the guide's section map); others refer to it briefly, if at all.

## Explaining numbers

- Every score, count or duration is followed by what it means: compared with same-age peers in plain words, or as what the child does or cannot do, where and with whom. A plain comparison helps the reader: "about 1 in 100 children his age would be expected to score this low or lower", when the report's figures support it.
- Give a number once, in the section that owns it. Another section that needs it names the behavior or the area and, at most, the one number its own point depends on.
- A sentence with three numbers or more and no clause saying what they mean is a list; rewrite it.

## What she avoids

- Blaming the family. Describe what the caregivers do now (giving in to end an outburst, keeping the child home) as a plain fact, without judgment; the plan's job is to give them other options.
- Treating harmless repetitive movement as a problem. Movement or stereotypy is a concern only when the sources show it interrupting learning, a transition, a shared activity or safety.
- Making speech, eye contact or an exact phrase a condition for anything; a request can be made by sign, gesture, picture or words.
- Promising outcomes. Say what ABA will teach and how progress will be measured, not that the child will improve.
- Filler ("it is important to note", "overall", "in conclusion"), hedging stacks ("may possibly suggest") and superlatives ("extremely").
- Comments on the evidence or on the assessment itself, such as "These are school goals, not evidence that he has met them" or "Decisions will depend on whether gains hold, not on the dates of those changes". State the fact or the criterion.
- Sentences about the text ("This section describes ...") or addressed to her or to the reader.

## Terms

- Diagnoses, instruments, scales and levels exactly as the sources and score reports write them.
- Behaviors by the names in her list of target behaviors, the same in every section, with only the first word capitalized. When the sources use two names for one behavior, use hers and keep it. Her current names include "Elopement" (or "Bolting" for running off during shared activities), kept apart from "Leaving task area"; "Non-compliant behavior"; "Tantrum"; "Physical aggression"; "Property destruction"; "Self injury behavior"; "Repetitive motor behavior". When her list names the behavior differently, her list wins.
- Procedures by their standard behavior-analytic names (functional communication training, differential reinforcement of alternative behavior, the Premack principle, behavioral skills training), spelled out on first use with the abbreviation after it when she uses one.

## Length

Each section's rules give its usual length from her texts. Shorter is better when nothing is lost: a section is too long when it retells what another section owns.
