# What the Florida payers look for

These are facts from the Florida Medicaid coverage policy and Molina Healthcare of Florida's authorization guide that bear on whether ABA hours are authorized. Every statement below comes from one of those two documents; the source is named in each heading. Use them to decide what to emphasize and what to flag as missing. Do not quote the policy or cite its section numbers in the assessment; the reviewer knows the policy.

## Who qualifies (Florida Medicaid policy 2.2)

Recipients under 21 who need medically necessary behavior analysis services to address behavior that impairs their ability to perform a major life activity. The policy recognizes functional impairment expressed through:

- Safety: aggression, self-injury, property destruction, elopement.
- Communication: problems with expressive or receptive language, poor understanding or use of nonverbal communication, stereotyped or repetitive language.
- Self-stimulating behavior: abnormal, inflexible or intense preoccupations.
- Self-care: difficulty recognizing risk or danger, grooming, eating, toileting.
- Other behaviors, including complexity of treatment, programming or environmental variables.

Our advice, not payer text: describing the child's impairment in these same terms helps the reviewer map the child onto the criteria.

The recipient must be referred by an independent physician or child psychologist, with a physician's order for behavior analysis and a Comprehensive Diagnostic Evaluation (CDE) led by a licensed practitioner. The CDE must include assessment findings and treatment recommendations for this child.

## Required standardized instruments (policy 4.2.1; Molina section 4)

- Vineland-3 Comprehensive Parent/Caregiver Interview Form for all recipients, plus the Maladaptive Behavior domain for ages 3 and older.
- BASC-3 Parenting Relationship Questionnaire (PRQ) for ages 2 through 18.
- The complete score reports, with outcome measure scores, are submitted with the authorization request.

Molina describes their role: the Vineland-3 and BASC-3 PRQ establish global adaptive functioning (communication, daily living, socialization), identify co-occurring emotional or behavioral concerns at a broad level, and support the medical necessity determination. They are not a functional behavior assessment: they do not capture behavior-specific, real-time data and are never used to build the FBA or the behavior plan. A CDE that happens to contain Vineland or BASC scores does not replace them.

## Why requests are returned or denied (Molina section 2)

Molina lists these under "CDE-related snags":

- Functional impairment described in only one setting, or not at all; impairment shown across home, school and community gives the complete clinical picture.
- Insufficient background and history: a CDE that reads as generic or template-based rather than individualized.
- Treatment recommendations not individualized to the member; overly brief reports.
- Diagnosis not stated explicitly by a clinician (scoring tools alone are not a diagnosis), not connected to DSM-5 criteria with examples, or without a severity level.
- No direct observation by the evaluating clinician; alternatives to behavior analysis not considered.

And under "authorization request snags":

- A behavior's function named without this member's data. The documentation must show how often the behavior occurs, at what rate or duration, and at what severity, with its impact. Function definitions without member-specific measurement do not establish severity or support the requested intensity.
- Parent or guardian participation not documented; if a parent cannot participate, this must be documented with an explanation and a plan to mitigate the impact.
- Other services not listed, or the request not explaining how behavior analysis complements rather than duplicates them.

## What makes the requested intensity credible (Molina section 5)

- The current level of functioning in adaptive behavior, communication, social skills, self-care and safety, informed by the Vineland-3 and BASC-3 scores.
- Scope: comprehensive treatment across several developmental domains typically requires greater intensity than focused treatment of specific skills or behaviors.
- Evidence of impairment across settings: school observations or teacher reports, the IEP, progress reports from other providers, the parents' accounts of home, community and social settings, and direct observation across settings and times of day.
- The member's actual availability (school hours, other therapies, family availability) and the caregivers' capacity to participate and carry out strategies.

## Other facts

- Behavior analysis services must reduce maladaptive behaviors and help the recipient reach the best possible functional level. The policy covers up to 40 hours per week of intervention (policy 4.2.2); for recipients under 21, services beyond that can be approved when medically necessary (policy, EPSDT paragraph).
- The parent or guardian should participate in treatment when possible and clinically appropriate; if they cannot, the reasons, the potential impact and how it is mitigated must be documented. Family adaptive behavior treatment guidance (caregiver training) is a covered service (policy 4.2.2).
- Seclusion and manual, mechanical or chemical restraint are not covered, nor are 1:1 aide, companion, chaperone, shadow or childcare services (policy 5.2).
- Discharge criteria must be objective and individualized; "when clinically appropriate" is not a criterion (Molina section 8).

## What the assessment and the behavior plan must contain (policy 4.2.1 and 6.2.2)

- The assessment must identify the behavioral deficits that interfere with a major life activity, including the events and the interactions that follow them which elicit and sustain each targeted behavior: its antecedents and its maintaining consequences.
- The behavior plan names intervention strategies likely to eliminate, mitigate or replace each behavior enough to re-engage the recipient in the major life activity. Each goal has its intervention strategies, a timeframe long enough to address the behavior, and how progress will be reported. The plan covers the requested authorization period (up to six months).
- Contents the policy lists: reason for referral; medical and developmental history, including medications prescribed for behavior; relevant family history; clinical interview; review of recent assessments and reports; assessment procedures and results; treatment settings.
- For each target, goal or objective: a definition in observable, measurable terms; how it is observed and measured; the current level (baseline); the reduction or acquisition procedures; the conditions under which it is shown and the mastery criteria; the date of introduction; the estimated date of mastery; and a plan for generalization.
- Caregiver training: proposed targets and objectives, training procedures, date of introduction and estimated date of mastery.
- The number of units for each procedure code, with the medical necessity for the units requested.
- A supervision plan naming the authorized supervisor(s).
- Care coordination with parents and caregivers, schools, state disability programs and others as applicable.
- A transition (fading) plan, a crisis management plan and a discharge plan.
- Services delivered in a school need the IEP; when there is no IEP or it holds no behavior analysis services, the request must justify the services and estimate when an IEP will be completed or updated (policy 7.2).

## The FBA and the behavior plan (Molina section 4, Part C)

- The FBA uses both direct methods (observation) and indirect ones (interviews, record review).
- Each targeted behavior has an operational definition with this member's baseline: frequency (how often), rate (how quickly it repeats), duration, and intensity or severity with the resulting harm. "The function alone is not an assessment."
- Hypothesized functions of each behavior, including its antecedents and consequences.
- Functionally equivalent replacement behaviors are identified.
- The behavior intervention plan is individualized to the member, the implementers and the setting, considers the stakeholders' ability to carry out the interventions, and targets the replacement behaviors in the skill acquisition part.
- Treatment goals that are not observable and measurable are a reason for return: each needs a definition in observable terms, baseline data, mastery criteria and an anticipated date of mastery (Molina section 2).

## Schedule and place of service (Molina section 3)

- The initial request includes a proposed weekly schedule with every behavior analysis code, its frequency and units, reflecting the member's actual availability, and the place(s) of service (home, clinic, school).
- Molina's own example of a schedule that may signal a clinical concern: an RBT scheduled from 3 to 9 PM after a full school day.
- Hours requested cannot exceed the hours the member is actually available (Molina section 5).

## Transition and discharge (Molina section 8)

- The transition and discharge plan is set at the start of services, not deferred, and is reviewed throughout treatment.
- Discharge criteria per AHCA policy: the member no longer meets medical necessity criteria; no longer engages in maladaptive behaviors; data show the frequency and severity of maladaptive behaviors no longer pose a barrier to major life activities; the level of functional impairment no longer justifies services; or the parent or guardian withdraws consent.
- A strong transition plan has measurable goals for the skills needed to move to a lower level of care, the caregiver's included; how hours will be titrated as those goals are met; community resources and supports to keep the gains after discharge; and more frequent caregiver training as the member approaches the transition criteria.
- Criteria must be objective and individualized to the member's current skills. Molina's example: "0 instances of aggression for 6 months when current levels are 50 instances/day". "When clinically appropriate" is not a criterion.

## What each section must show

Our mapping of the facts above onto the agency's sections, not payer text.

- Background Information: reason for referral and who referred; the diagnosis as the clinician states it; medical and developmental history with the medications prescribed for behavior; relevant family history; school and its plan; every other service the child receives.
- Medical Necessity Criteria: the Vineland-3 and BASC-3 PRQ results explained; impairment in more than one setting, with the kind of evidence for each; this child's own data on the behaviors (how often, how long, how severe, with what harm); why ABA, and how it complements rather than duplicates the other services.
- The behavior sections (maladaptive and replacement behaviors, assessments conducted, hypothesis based interventions, interventions): each behavior with its observable definition, measurement, baseline, antecedents, consequences and hypothesized function with its evidence; functionally equivalent replacement behaviors; strategies for each goal that the named implementers can carry out.
- Family/Caregiver Involvement: who takes part; their capacity to carry out the strategies; when a parent cannot take part, the reason, the likely impact and how it is mitigated.
- Generalization and Maintenance: how each skill is generalized across settings and people.
- Discharge and Service Fading Plan: objective, individualized criteria per behavior, skill and caregiver; how hours step down; community resources; caregiver training increasing near transition.
- Crisis Plan: the crisis management plan, with no seclusion or restraint.
- Summary and Recommendations: units per code with their medical necessity; place of service; a weekly schedule within the child's availability; the supervisor named.

## Payer readiness list

What a reviewer checks before reading closely. When the assessment lacks one of these and the sources do not give it, the gap stays visible as [MISSING: ...] in the section that owns it.

1. The diagnosis stated by a qualified clinician, with its severity, and the referral and physician's order.
2. Vineland-3 (with the Maladaptive Behavior domain from age 3) and BASC-3 PRQ (ages 2 to 18) results.
3. Impairment shown in at least two settings, each with whose evidence it is.
4. Every targeted behavior with an observable definition, frequency or rate, duration, intensity and harm, baseline, antecedents, consequences and hypothesized function with its evidence.
5. A functionally equivalent replacement behavior for each function.
6. Every other service listed, and how ABA complements rather than duplicates it.
7. Caregiver participation, or the reason it is limited, its impact and the mitigation.
8. Objective, individualized discharge criteria and a step-down plan.
9. A crisis plan with no seclusion or restraint.
10. Units per code justified, a schedule within the child's availability, and the supervisor named.
