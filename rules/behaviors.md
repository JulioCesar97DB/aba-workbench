# Target behaviors: definitions, functions and groups

## Which behaviors

Every behavior that a caregiver, a teacher, her notes, her sections, her list or the BCBA's documents report as a concern goes in, not only the ones that interfere most. A behavior that only the observations show, and that nobody names as a concern, is an open item, not a target. Never add a behavior no source reports.

- **How many.** She writes 8 to 14 behaviors.
- **Names.** Use her names first (the ones her sections and her list use: elopement, non-compliant behavior, tantrum, off task behavior, repetitive motor behavior, screaming, mouthing, self injury behavior, physical aggression, property destruction, invading personal space, echolalia, and the like), first word capitalized. Prefer the generic name that covers the most responses of this child: throwing, knocking over and overturning things are one Property destruction. Propose another name only when none of hers fits.
- **Fewer than 8.** Split a generic behavior by her split rule (a different measure, a different safety response, a passive form) until there are 8, and only into behaviors the sources show this child doing. When the sources do not give 8, keep what they give and add an open item; never add a behavior to reach the number.
- **More than 14.** Merge by her merge rule (the same function and the same measure) until there are 14 at most; when that is not enough, add an open item asking her which to keep.
- **Her list is final.** Never merge two behaviors she listed separately into one, and never split one of hers: if you think two of hers are one response class, say so in a group (below), not by merging them. The splitting and merging above apply only to the behaviors you name from the sources.
- **Precursors** (whining before a tantrum, for example) are never a target of their own: when the sources show one, write it inside the definition of the behavior it leads to. The sessions are usually too short to show precursors, so do not look for one the sources do not give.

## A definition is what this child does

A reviewer and an RBT must be able to count the behavior from the definition alone, and it must describe this child, not a textbook.

- **What the child does.** Start from the topographies the sources record for this child: the movements, the words, the objects, the people they are aimed at. List them first, each in its own line, each with a quote from the incident that shows it. Write the definition only from those topographies. A form of the behavior that no source records for this child does not go in; "hitting, kicking or pushing" is wrong for a child who only pushes.
- **Something the child does (the dead-man test).** Every definition names at least one observable, active response the child makes: something an observer sees or hears (says "no", walks away, keeps playing, pushes the materials away, cries). A definition a dead man could meet, because it names only an absence ("stops interacting", "does nothing to begin the task", "is not attending", "refuses" with nothing the child does), is what her agency's QA and the payers return most often. An absence or a latency ("does not begin within 5 seconds of the instruction") goes in only together with the observable forms the sources record for this child: "does not begin the task within 5 seconds and instead says no, walks away or keeps playing". When the sources record no form at all, write the latency with a `[MISSING: what the child does instead]` marker and an open item; never invent a form.
- **The opening.** One sentence that says what counts, beginning with what an instance is ("Any instance in which [the child] …"; "Any episode in which …" for something that lasts; "Any period in which …" for something timed).
- **Onset and offset.** When an instance starts and when it ends, as observable events. For a behavior counted by episodes, the offset is a stretch of time without the behavior.
- **Counting.** Whether each discrete instance counts on its own, or a continuous episode counts once; say which.
- **Exclusions.** When two of the target behaviors could score the same response (a scream inside a tantrum, under Tantrum and Screaming; pushing the materials away, under Task refusal and Property destruction), write in each definition which one it belongs to, naming the other behavior by its name, so the two exclusions point to each other and a single event never counts toward two behaviors. The same example never goes under two behaviors, and a topography of one behavior named in another's definition needs that exclusion.
- **Wording.** The name, the definition, the examples and the non-examples are pasted into ABA Matrix as they are, so they follow the style guide: no dates, no names of caregivers or professionals, no quoted speech (paraphrase what the child said), no long dashes.
- **Examples.** Two or three short scenes of this child from the sources, each opening with the child's name, each with its quote in the claim's ref (the example itself paraphrases). Together they cover the functions the behavior may serve (a behavior usually has one to three): when the behavior is maintained by escape and by attention, one example shows each. Fewer when the sources record fewer incidents; never invent one to reach the number.
- **Non-examples.** Three. One may be the appropriate alternative the plan will teach (a request for help or a break, going with permission): it marks the behavior's boundary, not an incident, so the sources need not show it. The others come from what the sources show of this child: accidental or harmless forms. A repetitive movement during free time that interrupts nothing is a non-example. Fewer when the sources do not give them; never invent an incident to reach the number.
- **Measure.** Frequency by default, for every behavior, because it keeps the plan simple to carry out. Duration only for a tantrum whose episodes are long. Time sampling only for a behavior whose rate is so high that it cannot be counted; the measure list has no time sampling yet, so keep frequency and write the proposal as the measure's `check`. Latency is not one of her defaults.

### Thresholds

The timing and distance thresholds in a definition (how many seconds count as not following an instruction, how far from the adult counts as leaving, how long without the behavior ends an episode) are her standard practice. Her defaults, confirmed in her questionnaire answers:

- Following an instruction: the child starts what was asked within 5 seconds; no start within 5 seconds counts as not following it.
- Leaving the supervising adult (elopement): more than 6 feet away from the supervising adult without permission.
- Off task: 3 seconds or more without attending to the task.
- Repetitive movements (stereotypy): counted only when they interrupt a task, a transition or play with others for 3 seconds or longer; when they interrupt nothing, they are not counted.
- End of an episode: the behavior has stopped for 10 seconds in a row; for a tantrum, 30 seconds.

What overrides a default, first to last: a threshold her sections give for this child, then one the sources give for this child, then one in `<standing-instructions>`. Her standing instructions override one threshold at a time: a line of hers takes the place of the default it changes (the same label, such as "Off task"), and every default she does not change still applies. Every threshold is adapted to the child's age and level of functioning: when the sources show that a default does not fit this child (a very young child, or a child whose instructions take longer to process), keep the default and write in the definition's `check` what the sources show and that she may want to adapt it. Never pick a number of your own. When a definition needs a threshold that neither her defaults nor the other places give (how far from the assigned spot counts as leaving the task area, for example), write a marker in its place, `[MISSING: your threshold for …]`. The app refuses a number of seconds, feet or minutes in a definition, onset or offset that no quote gives, unless it is her default or her own threshold for that place, with its unit: 5 seconds or 3 seconds or 6 feet for the start, 10 seconds or 6 feet for the end, 30 seconds for the end of a tantrum. A default's number is never a count.

## Baseline

The baseline is the count (or minutes) in each of her direct observations, each with its date, setting and length, exactly as the notes give them. The length is written as the notes write it ("3:30 to 5:30 p.m.", "2 hours"); the app works out the minutes. Report-based estimates (a parent's "several a day", a school's weekly count) go in `other`, attributed. Never compute a mean or a rate: the app does that from the counts.

- **Duration.** A duration baseline is given either as seconds or minutes per incident (the length of each episode) or as total minutes per session; write which one, as the notes give it.
- **Reported but not observed.** A behavior the caregiver reports that did not occur in any of the three sessions has no observations; its baseline is the caregiver's count, in `other`, attributed to the caregiver, with the period the caregiver gave ("about 4 a week"), and its `check` says that it did not occur in the three sessions. When the caregiver gives no count, add an open item asking for it.

## Antecedents and consequences

From the ABC data and the observation notes: what happened right before (the demand, the transition, the item removed, the adult turning away) and right after (the demand stopped, the item given, attention, a reprimand), with the counts the data give ("6 of 8 episodes").

## The function is a hypothesis with its evidence

Use her five functions: tangibles, attention, escape, sensory, automatic.

- List each piece of evidence on its own: the tool (FAST, MAS, ABC data, direct observation, interview, one of her sections), the respondent, the result exactly as recorded, and which functions it supports.
- **Primary**: a function that direct data (the ABC data or the observation) and at least one indirect tool (the FAST, the MAS, the interview) both support.
- **Secondary**: a function one source supports, or that the data support less strongly.
- When the tools disagree (the mother's FAST points to escape, the grandmother's MAS to attention), state each result with its respondent in the evidence, choose nothing that the direct data do not support, and write the disagreement as the function's `check`. Never average scores across tools or respondents.
- A function supported by one indirect tool alone is not primary; say in the reasoning that only that tool supports it.
- When no function meets the bar for primary, leave primary empty, name the best-supported candidate in the reasoning, and add an open item asking her which function is primary.
- **Reasoning**: one to three sentences on how the evidence combines. Write the function as what maintains the behavior ("is maintained by escape from demands"), never as "hypothesized".

## Groups

A group gathers behaviors that will share one consequence procedure later. Her rule: **the behaviors share a response class, a function and a measure.** The group's function is a primary function of every member, and its measure is every member's measure; the app checks both.

- Keep behaviors apart when the measure differs (a counted behavior and a timed one), when they need different safety responses (a dangerous behavior and a mild one), or when one is a passive form of another.
- Never group across functions.
- A behavior belongs to one group at most; a behavior alone needs no group.
- Each group gives its nature, its function, its measure and a reason of one sentence ("none" for a function or a measure that cannot be named). Groups are hypotheses: she approves them.

## Barriers and risks

- **Barriers**: choose from her fixed list only (safety, communication, social interaction, following instructions, learning and task engagement, daily living skills, community participation, transitions and flexibility, emotional regulation), one to four per behavior. A dangerous behavior gets safety. Barriers are `proposed`.
- **Risks** (the risks part): each risk the sources show, the behaviors it comes from, and its evidence (incidents, injuries, where and when). A level only when a source or her text gives one.

## An example

One behavior from a case map, for an invented child, Nadia. Her default gives the offset threshold (10 seconds); the counts and incidents come from her father's interview and the direct observation of 05/14/2026. Never reuse its sentences, names, dates or numbers.

> Name: Climbing furniture
>
> What she does: climbs onto the kitchen counter from a chair; climbs the living-room bookshelf by its shelves; stands on the top of the sofa back.
>
> Definition: Any instance in which Nadia raises both feet off the floor onto furniture not made for sitting or lying on (the counter, the bookshelf, the sofa back, a table). Onset: her second foot leaves the floor or the chair. Offset: both feet are back on the floor and she stays off the furniture for 10 seconds. Each climb is counted separately. Exclusions: climbing on playground equipment, and sitting or lying on the sofa or a bed.
>
> Examples: Nadia climbed the bookshelf during direct observation in the home, within a minute of her father saying that the markers were put away. Nadia climbs from a kitchen chair onto the counter to reach the art supplies on top of the refrigerator.
>
> Non-examples: Nadia asks her father to get the markers down. Nadia climbs the ladder of the slide at the park. Nadia sits on the sofa to read.
>
> Measure: frequency. Baseline: 3 climbs in the observation of 05/14/2026 (length: 2 hours).

What makes it work: every form of the behavior in the definition is one the sources record for Nadia; onset, offset and the counting rule can be observed; the exclusions keep it apart from play that is safe; the examples are her own incidents, each opening with her name, and the first non-example is the request the plan will teach, which marks the boundary; the threshold is her default, not a number of the model's own.
